Regulatory Approach and Compliance Policy

Contents
Example 1USED

Learning why CAV looks deeper before sending renters to VCAT

Situation

Around May 2025, when I was new to the Enquiries Officer role, I advised a renter with an urgent repairs issue to apply straight to VCAT. My team manager picked this up in a QA review.

Task

My manager explained I should have looked at the matter further, including whether it suited dispute resolution, and asked me to get familiar with CAV's Regulatory Approach and Compliance Policy.

Action
  • I studied the policy straight after the review.
  • It showed me that CAV's role goes beyond each person's outcome: even when we can't get the renter the result they want, digging further gives CAV intelligence about possible breaches that affect other renters.
  • I changed my practice. I now ask more questions and consider dispute resolution before pointing someone to VCAT, while always confirming VCAT remains open to them.
Result

I understand why the quicker option isn't always the right one for CAV as a regulator, and I apply this regularly. For example, about two months ago I explained this reasoning to a complainant who wanted to go straight to VCAT about a motor car trader, and the matter was referred to intelligence.

Example 2USED

Choosing a proportionate response to a mould repair complaint

Situation

Around June 2026, as an Escalations Officer, I picked up a case from a renter who said their rental provider was refusing to carry out urgent mould repairs.

Task

I needed to assess whether there was enough basis for dispute resolution or a referral for compliance action, and choose the right regulatory response.

Action
  • I called the renter to understand the issue and asked them to send everything relevant: emails between the parties, photos and the rental agreement.
  • When I reviewed the documents, I couldn't find any point where the mould had been raised with the rental provider. The emails they forwarded were about a different repair.
  • I called the renter back to clarify. They confirmed they hadn't reported the mould. Their belief that the rental provider would refuse was based on past delays.
  • I explained that dispute resolution or a compliance referral wasn't appropriate yet, because under the Act the renter first has to tell the rental provider about the repair. I explained that CAV takes an intelligence-led, risk-based approach and focuses its action where there is evidence of a breach.
  • I explained what the Act says about urgent repairs, starting with the renter's obligation to tell the rental provider. I gave clear next steps: notify the rental provider in writing and, if it wasn't resolved, contact me again for assessment or apply to RDRV, which can refer it on to VCAT.
Result

The renter understood their own obligation and had a clear, documented path forward. CAV's resources weren't spent on a dispute the rental provider hadn't been given a chance to resolve, and the renter knew they could come straight back to me if the rental provider didn't act.

Contents
Example 1USED

Referring a possibly overseas-based retailer to intelligence

Same story also used for KSC 7 Promote Inclusion, Ex 1

Situation

Around October 2026, I handled a complaint about an outstanding refund from an online retailer, Bondi Babes. From previous cases, I knew the business rarely responded to CAV, and CAV had never been able to find an ABN for it. It promotes itself as an Australian company, but it doesn't appear to issue invoices and may be operating from overseas.

Task

I needed to try to resolve the complaint, and then decide what should happen when dispute resolution failed.

Action
  • I contacted the retailer and set realistic expectations with the complainant early.
  • When there was no response, I gave the complainant their remaining options: a chargeback through their financial institution, and independent legal advice. I explained that VCAT may not be practical without an identifiable Australian business.
  • Under our case guidelines, I referred the business to the Intelligence branch, highlighting that it presents as Australian but may not be.
Result

The complaint wasn't resolved, but the complainant had clear next steps, and the business is on record with intelligence to inform any wider action. My lesson: an unresolved case still has value when it's escalated through the right channel.

Example 2USED

Escalating a repeat non-lodgement of bond to the Renting Taskforce

Situation

Around May 2026, as an Escalations Officer, I picked up a complaint from a resident who had lived in a rooming house. After moving out, they tried to get their bond refunded and found it didn't seem to have been lodged with the RTBA.

Task

I needed to resolve the resident's bond issue and assess whether the matter needed escalating beyond this one complaint.

Action
  • I checked CAV's records and found previous cases against the same rooming house operator for the same issue, which pointed to a pattern.
  • I completed dispute resolution with the operator and followed up until I could confirm the bond had been lodged, which took a few days.
  • I weighed up the evidence. The history showed the operator knew the law but was only lodging bonds once a complaint was made. That pointed to possible wilful non-compliance, not a one-off mistake.
  • Under the Escalation Framework, I escalated the case to the Renting Taskforce, because not lodging bonds is one of the offences it targets. I included the case history and the evidence of the pattern from the earlier complaints.
Result

The bond was lodged within days, so the resident could pursue their refund. The operator's pattern of conduct went to the team best placed to take compliance action, which protects future rooming house residents, not just this one. Taskforce outcomes aren't shared back to Escalations Officers, so I don't know the final result.

Example 1USED

Applying the consumer guarantees to a supplier dispute

Same story also used for KSC 6 Working Collaboratively, Ex 1

Situation

Around September 2026, a newer colleague asked for help with a complex case. A business was refusing a remedy under the consumer guarantees by claiming it might not have been the supplier.

Task

I needed to apply my knowledge of the Australian Consumer Law so my colleague could take the right next step.

Action
  • I identified that who the supplier is decides who has to provide the remedy, so that fact had to be confirmed first.
  • I advised my colleague to ask the complainant for an invoice or receipt to establish where the product came from.
  • I pointed them to the relevant sections of the Australian Consumer Law that would apply depending on what the receipt showed.
Result

My colleague had a clear, legally sound plan before taking any further action, rather than deciding on incomplete facts.

Example 2USED

Explaining the corded window covering standard to an agent

Situation

Around February 2026, an estate agent contacted CAV about the rental minimum standards. They wanted a definite answer on whether the corded internal window coverings (blind cords) at a property were compliant.

Task

I needed to give them accurate information about what the standard requires, without crossing into legal advice by ruling on their specific property.

Action
  • I went to the source: Schedule 4, clause 15 of the Residential Tenancies Regulations 2021, which has applied since 1 December 2025. I read the standard to them word for word so there was no room for misinterpretation.
  • In summary, a loose cord must not be able to form a loop of 220 mm or longer below 1600 mm above floor level. It must be secured by a cord guide or a cleat (a cleat at least 1600 mm above the floor), installed according to the window covering's installation instructions.
  • I explained why I couldn't tell them whether their particular blinds complied: interpreting the standard for a specific property would be legal advice, and CAV provides legal information.
  • I gave them clear pathways: discuss it with their officer in effective control, engage a business that does rental minimum standards inspections, or get independent legal advice.
Result

The agent left the call with the exact wording of the standard and three ways to get a definitive answer. They weren't happy, because they wanted me to just give them the answer. I held the line between information and advice and pointed them to the CAV feedback form so their dissatisfaction could be formally heard.

Example 1USED

Explaining to a frustrated complainant why dispute resolution was still worth doing

Same story also used for KSC 14 Influence and Persuasion, Ex 1

Situation

Around September 2026, as an Escalations Officer, I picked up a complaint about a motor car trader. The complainant had been trying to resolve faults in their car with the trader for about six months, and the trader refused to accept responsibility. The complainant wanted to go straight to VCAT. They had also seen many similar complaints about the trader in online reviews.

Task

I needed to respect their right to go to VCAT, and also help them see why CAV contacting the trader first was still worthwhile.

Action
  • I confirmed they could apply to VCAT at any time.
  • I explained that contacting the trader would let CAV gather more information and educate the trader on its obligations under the Australian Consumer Law and the Motor Car Traders Act.
  • I linked their case to the bigger picture. Given the pattern in the online reviews, even if we couldn't get them a personal outcome, the extra time would give CAV intelligence that could improve the trader's conduct and the market for other consumers.
  • With their agreement, I went ahead with dispute resolution. The trader engaged, and I explained their obligations to them.
Result
  • The trader kept its position, so the complainant didn't get a personal remedy and went on to apply to VCAT.
  • The trader received education on its obligations.
  • I referred the matter to the Intelligence branch, so the pattern is now on record for possible compliance action.
  • My lesson: even an unsuccessful case can have value for the community, and helping complainants see that keeps them engaged with the process.

Example 2USED

Sharing a pattern with the Intelligence branch about a motor car trader

Same story also used for KSC 5 Outcomes Thinking, Ex 2

Situation

Around July 2026, as an Escalations Officer, I handled a complaint about a licensed motor car trader. The consumer's used car developed faults within six months of purchase. Through dispute resolution, I got the trader and the consumer to agree on a repair.

Task

While resolving the case, I reviewed the trader's previous cases and saw a pattern. I needed to decide whether it should go beyond my own case, and get it to the right area.

Action

Example 1USED

Adjusting how I communicated for a complainant with a condition affecting their speech

Same story also used for KSC 2 Enforcement Activities and the Escalation Framework, Ex 1

Situation

Around October 2026, as an Escalations Officer, I picked up an online complaint about an outstanding refund from an online retailer, Bondi Babes. The complainant explained that they live with a condition that affects their speech, and that they preferred email to phone.

Task

I needed to make sure they could fully access CAV's dispute resolution service on their terms, while still getting the information I needed to act on the complaint in good time.

Action
  • I respected their stated preference and did all my contact by email at first.
  • Responses were slow; the first took about three weeks. Instead of closing the case, I offered a choice: a short phone call, with someone they trusted speaking for them, so I could ask all my questions at once and act sooner. I made clear it was an option, not a requirement.
  • They chose the call. They confirmed their identity, as our privacy requirements say, and gave consent before handing over to their support person, who answered my questions.
  • I contacted the retailer. Based on previous cases with this business, I set realistic expectations with the complainant early that a response might not come.
  • When the retailer didn't respond, I explained the complainant's remaining options: a chargeback through their financial institution, and independent legal advice. I also explained why VCAT may not be practical, since CAV hadn't been able to find an ABN and the business may be operating from overseas.
  • The business promotes itself as Australian, so I referred it to the Intelligence branch under our case guidelines.
Result
  • The complainant got full access to the service through the adjustments they chose, and had clear next steps even though the retailer never responded.
  • The business is now on record with the Intelligence branch.
  • My lesson: an inclusive approach means asking what works for the person and offering options, not deciding for them, and it can be done while still meeting CAV's privacy and process requirements.
Example 2USED

Adapting communication for a renter new to Australia

Same story also used for KSC 11 Managing Difficult Conversations, Ex 2

Situation

Around mid-October 2026, I picked up a case from a renter who had recently moved to Australia, with several complaints about their real estate agency. English wasn't their first language, and they felt the agency, and at first CAV, weren't taking them seriously.

Task

I needed to make sure they could fully understand CAV's advice and process, and felt heard, even though some of their matters were outside what dispute resolution could cover.

Action
  • Our first phone call was difficult because of the language barrier, so I offered an interpreter.
  • They turned it down and said writing was easier, because they could translate it at their own pace. I respected that and moved all further contact to email.
  • I wrote in plain English and avoided jargon, especially when explaining CAV's regulatory approach and why we can't act as a personal advocate. Those concepts are easy to misread in translation.
  • I set out clearly what I could do (dispute resolution on the unsigned lease and a referral to the Renting Taskforce) and what to expect, including that Taskforce referrals don't come with updates.
Result

The renter understood CAV's role and accepted the plan. Their frequent, frustrated emails stopped, and they felt their concerns were being taken seriously. My lesson: inclusion means offering adjustments like interpreters, but also letting the person choose what actually works for them.

If asked about correcting non-inclusive behaviour

I haven't had to at CAV. If I heard a colleague make an assumption about a customer because of their background, I'd raise it with them privately and respectfully, explain the impact, and escalate to my manager if it continued. Our VPS values of respect and human rights make that everyone's responsibility.

Example 1USED

Feedback on a misrouted complaint, which led to a clearer wiki article

Same story also used for KSC 9 Innovation and Continuous Improvement, Ex 2

Situation

Around late 2025, as an Enquiries Officer, I took a call from a complainant whose previous case had just been closed by an Escalations Officer. Their new issue looked related, so I left a PROC note on the closed case asking the Escalations Officer to call them back. If I had asked more questions, I would have found it was actually a separate complaint.

Task

The Escalations Officer raised it with my team manager, who told me it should have been lodged as a new complaint rather than a PROC note. I needed to take that feedback on board and make sure it didn't happen again.

Action
  • I listened to my manager's feedback and accepted where I'd gone wrong. I should have asked more questions before deciding the issue was related.
  • I also gave honest feedback in return, without being defensive: I showed him that the wording in our wiki seemed to point to a PROC note as the right action in this situation.
  • I apologised to the Escalations Officer for the extra work.
  • I changed how I handle these calls straight away. When a complainant has a recently closed case, I now probe further to check whether the issue is actually new before choosing how to lodge it.
Result
  • The wiki guidance was rewritten to be clearer, which reduces the risk of other officers making the same mistake.
  • I haven't repeated the error.
  • Several team managers have since told me in catch-ups that they're impressed by how quickly I take on and apply feedback.
  • My lesson: criticism is information. Understanding it, rather than arguing, is how I improve, and raising a gap respectfully can improve things for the whole team.
Example 2USED

Questioning a QA score on a call with a swearing complainant

Situation

Around late 2025, as an Enquiries Officer, I was marked down in a quality assurance review. The complainant had sworn throughout the call and I hadn't asked them to stop. I was personally comfortable with it, because the language wasn't directed at me and seemed to be just how they spoke.

Task

I needed to understand why I had been marked down and decide how to respond to the feedback.

Action
  • I questioned the score in the call review. I explained my view that the swearing came from the complainant, and asked to understand why it counted against my call.
  • I listened to the reasoning. Letting it go gives the complainant the impression that this language is acceptable across CAV. Colleagues they speak to next may not be comfortable with it, and as a government department we need to set appropriate expectations.
  • Once I understood the broader impact on my colleagues and on CAV's standards, I accepted the feedback.
  • Since then, when a caller uses inappropriate language, I respectfully ask them to tone it down and explain why.
Result

It hasn't come up as an issue in my QA reviews since. Customers I speak with now hear a consistent expectation, which should make calls easier for the colleagues they deal with next. My lesson: something that's fine for me isn't automatically fine for the team, and questioning feedback to understand it is different from rejecting it.

Example 1USED

Understanding what was driving a defensive bond respondent

Same story also used for KSC 11 Managing Difficult Conversations, Ex 1

Situation

In early November 2026, I called a respondent who had taken a bond from a renter she was subletting to but never lodged it with the RTBA. The agreement also wasn't on the prescribed form and included a prohibited late-rent interest term. She became defensive as soon as I explained why I was calling.

Task

I needed to understand why she had kept the bond, so I could explain her obligations in a way she would actually take on board.

Action
  • Before discussing the legislation, I asked her to explain the situation from her side.
  • That showed what was driving her: she felt entitled to the bond because the renter had left the property in a poor state, which cost her the bond on her own lease plus extra costs. She also wasn't familiar with renting laws.
  • I confirmed I understood her position, then changed my approach. Instead of starting with breaches, I started with what mattered to her: how she could recover her costs through a bond claim and RDRV, then VCAT if needed.
  • Once she could see I understood her concerns, I explained her obligations on the bond, the prescribed form and the interest term.
  • I followed up with an email summarising our conversation, with the relevant legislation, steps for lodging the bond and the prescribed form, so she had it in writing.
Result

She agreed to lodge the bond and pursue her costs the proper way, and she understood her other obligations. The call ended calmly and she thanked me. My lesson: once I understand what someone actually wants, I can frame compliance as a path to it rather than a penalty.

Example 2USED

Seeing overdue Samsung refunds from the consumer's point of view

Same story also used for KSC 15 Stakeholder Management, Ex 2

Situation

Around June 2026, I handled several complaints against Samsung Australia, mostly about overdue refunds. Consumers had been given a refund timeframe of roughly 14–24 business days and were frustrated when it passed.

Task

I needed to resolve the overdue refunds, and to work out why so many consumers were complaining about the same thing.

Action

In my lead case, where the business-day timeframe had genuinely passed, the consumer received their refund, and most of my other Samsung cases were resolved. Seeing the issue from the consumer's side helped explain the pattern, and the clear, steady communication helped build a relationship where Samsung's dispute resolution manager now contacts me proactively.

Managing Difficult Conversations

Contents
Example 1USED

Defensive respondent in a non-lodged bond complaint

Same story also used for KSC 10 Interpersonal Skills, Ex 1

Situation

In early November 2026, as an Escalations Officer at CAV, I was assigned a complaint about a non-lodged bond. The renter alleged the respondent had sublet the property to him and taken a bond but never lodged it with the RTBA. He had since moved out and couldn't reach her to get his bond back. When I reviewed the rental agreement he sent, I found two more issues: it wasn't on the prescribed form, and it had a term requiring interest on late rent, which is a prohibited term under the Residential Tenancies Act.

Task

My role was to contact the respondent, explain her obligations and get the bond lodged with the RTBA so the renter could recover it. I needed to stay impartial and encourage her to comply, not just list breaches.

Action
  • I introduced myself and explained why I was calling. She immediately became defensive, talked over me and seemed ready to hang up.
  • Instead of trying to take control of the call, I stopped talking and let her finish. Pushing back at that point would only have escalated things.
  • I explained that my role was to be impartial and make sure both parties understood their rights and obligations. Then I asked her to tell me what had happened from her side before we got into the legislation.
  • That clarified the real issue. She felt entitled to keep the bond because the renter had left the property in a poor state, which cost her the bond on her own lease plus extra costs. She also said she wasn't familiar with renting laws.
  • I started with her options rather than her breaches. She could lodge the bond and then make a bond claim, and she could seek further compensation through RDRV, and then VCAT if it isn't resolved. This was the turning point: she could see I wasn't there to take sides.
  • Once she was receptive, I explained her obligations: the bond must be lodged with the RTBA, the agreement must be on the prescribed form, and including a late-rent interest term may itself be a breach, even though she said she had never charged it.
  • After the call, I emailed her a summary of our conversation, the relevant sections of the legislation, step-by-step instructions for lodging the bond, and a copy of the prescribed rental agreement with a link to where it sits on the CAV website.
Result

The call ended calmly, and she agreed to lodge the bond and pursue her costs through a bond claim and RDRV instead of withholding the bond. She understood the prescribed form and prohibited-term requirements and thanked me for my help. The lesson I took away is that starting a respondent call by asking for their side, before discussing possible breaches, lowers defensiveness and leads to better compliance outcomes.

Example 2USED

Renter unhappy that their complaint was out of scope

Same story also used for KSC 7 Promote Inclusion, Ex 2

Situation

In mid-October 2026, as an Escalations Officer, I picked up a case from a renter who had recently moved to Australia. They had several complaints about their real estate agency: poor complaint handling, outstanding urgent repairs, entry without a valid notice of entry, and a lease the rental provider had never signed. They felt the agency wasn't taking them seriously. Several of these matters were already before VCAT, some heard and some not.

Task

I had to assess what CAV could appropriately act on and explain that clearly. That included telling them that dispute resolution generally isn't the right path for matters already at VCAT.

Action
  • My initial advice was that dispute resolution wasn't the best path forward. The renter was unhappy and felt I just wanted to close the case.
  • Rather than defending that position, I asked clarifying questions to separate what was at VCAT from what wasn't. That showed the unsigned lease was not part of their VCAT applications.
  • English wasn't their first language, so I offered an interpreter. They preferred written communication because it was easier for them to translate, so I switched to email for the rest of the case.
  • I explained CAV's Regulatory Approach and Compliance Policy in plain terms: CAV is the regulator, not a personal advocate, and can't guarantee an individual outcome, but complaints feed into how CAV targets compliance across the sector.
  • I agreed to two clear actions. First, dispute resolution on the unsigned lease: I contacted the agency's officer in effective control and sent written correspondence to pass on to the owners. Second, a referral of the remaining matters to the Renting Taskforce as possible breaches of the Act.
  • I set expectations up front: Taskforce referrals don't come with updates to the renter or to me.
Result
  • The officer in effective control confirmed she had passed the correspondence to the owners, and the case is ongoing while I wait for the signed agreement.
  • The agency's possible breaches were sent to the right area for compliance action.
  • The renter's frequent, frustrated emails stopped, and they accepted the outcome and CAV's role.
  • My lesson: get the full facts before giving a scope decision. Saying clearly what I can do, and not only what I can't, turns a rejection into a way forward.

Faulty used car bought privately, where jurisdiction was unclear

Example 2USED

Verifying a disputed vehicle model year (BYD)

Same story also used for KSC 14 Influence and Persuasion, Ex 2

Situation

Around June 2026, as an Escalations Officer, I received a complaint against BYD. The consumer said they had bought a 2026 model car but were supplied a 2025 model. Their paperwork said "2026 model", but nothing showed the model year of the car actually supplied.

Task

When I raised the complaint, BYD said the consumer was mistaken. I needed to decide whether to accept that or test it further, and no guideline covered how to verify a vehicle's model year.

Action
  • I raised the complaint with BYD first. They relied on their original advice that the consumer was incorrect.
  • Rather than close the case on BYD's word, I looked for an independent source myself and found the federal Register of Approved Vehicles (RAV) public search, which returns vehicle details from a VIN.
  • I entered the VIN, and the search showed a 2025 build date.
  • I weighed up what that did and didn't prove. Build date and model year aren't always the same, so on its own it didn't confirm a breach. But it was credible evidence that the complainant might be right, and it was worth putting to the supplier.
  • I went back to BYD with the paperwork and the RAV search result as evidence and asked them to reassess it themselves rather than accusing them of a breach.
  • I framed the potential issues under the Australian Consumer Law: the consumer guarantee that goods must match their description (s 56), and misleading or deceptive conduct (s 18).
Result

BYD investigated and offered the consumer three options: a full refund, a replacement vehicle, or a partial refund to keep the older model. The consumer chose a replacement and BYD supplied it. The case was resolved without the consumer needing to go to VCAT. I'd use the same approach in rent assessments: check a claim against an independent source, be clear about what the evidence does and doesn't prove, and then decide.

Example 2USED

Adapting to the RTBA system launch

Situation

In December 2025, when I was an Enquiries Officer, CAV launched the new RTBA website and system. Calls and contacts to the RTBA increased sharply. Management made the RTBA line the priority for everyone trained in it, so fewer staff were available for the other lines, such as residential tenancies and Australian Consumer Law, and demand and wait times on those lines rose sharply too.

Task

I wasn't RTBA-trained, so my job was to absorb the extra load on the other lines while keeping service quality up for callers who had already waited longer than usual.

Action
  • I understood the decision behind the pressure: management had concentrated trained staff where demand was most critical. My role was to support that by taking more of the load on the other lines, not to treat it as someone else's problem.
  • I prepared a short, consistent explanation for callers who were frustrated about wait times, so I could acknowledge their frustration, explain the higher demand, and move on to their enquiry quickly.
  • I changed my note-taking. I used to finalise notes after each call; I switched to completing them during the call, which cut my after-call time so I was ready for the next caller sooner.
Result

I was able to handle calls and complaints more quickly through the peak, and my call quality and QA results didn't drop. The note-taking change has stayed part of how I work.

If asked: “What pressures do you see facing this team?”

  • Demand and housing pressure. Renters are under cost pressure, which drives requests for rent increase assessments and investigations. CAV has a legislative responsibility to investigate and report on those requests in a timely way.
  • Ongoing reform. Changes like the rental minimum standards, for example the corded window covering standard from 1 December 2025, and the new RTBA system bring spikes in contact and mean frontline staff must keep their knowledge current.
  • Organisational change. The CAV contact centre now sits within DGS's Whole of Victorian Government contact centre structure, with a focus on digitising and streamlining services.
  • Flexibility. The role is tied to CAV's frontline response, so priorities can shift to answer inbound calls when demand peaks. I did exactly that during the RTBA launch.
